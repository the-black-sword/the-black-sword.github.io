The Library

Astronomy

Star atlases, observing guides, cosmology, and the classics from Ptolemy to Burnham.

Chosen from the shelf

15 books I would hand you first, and why.

  1. 365 Starry Nights and The Soul of the Night

    Chet Raymo

    An astronomy book almost as poetic as it is instructive. Pair it with The Stars by H. A. Rey and you have the perfect pair for learning the night sky.

  2. A Brief History of Time

    Stephen Hawking

    The book to start understanding how cosmologists see the Universe and our place in it.

  3. Burnham’s Celestial Handbook (three volumes)

    Robert Burnham Jr.

    The seminal guide for the citizen scientist-astronomer, one you’ll find yourself returning to again and again, even in the internet age. For searching out and planning observations, it is as edifying as it is fun.

  4. Black Holes and Time Warps

    Kip Thorne

    From the Nobel Prize–winning physicist who advised Christopher Nolan on how a black hole should look in Interstellar: the go-to voice on gravity, black holes and space-time.

  5. Exploring the Moon Through Binoculars and Small Telescopes

    Ernest Cherrington

    A wonderful guide to observing the Moon with binoculars and beginning the systematic study of its surface, known as selenography.

  6. Almagest

    Ptolemy

    The great work of the Greek astronomer of Alexandria. His geocentric system is surprisingly accurate, because his geometry, complex as it is, does what it was meant to do: predict where the planets will appear in the sky.

  7. Sidereus Nuncius

    Galileo Galilei

    Arguably the first book of telescopic astronomy. The Starry Messenger lets you share the wonder of the first man to write about what he saw through his little “spyglass,” and glimpse how it would change the way we see the world.

  8. Star Names: Their Lore and Meaning

    Richard Hinckley Allen

    Where the names of the stars come from (Polaris, Rigel, Vega, Betelgeuse) and their histories in Greek, Latin and, most often, Arabic. The names give us a glimpse of how our ancestors saw their place beneath the night sky.

  9. Structure and Evolution of the Stars

    Martin Schwarzschild

    The classic account of how stars live and die, the science behind every supernova. Written by a pioneer of stellar evolution, it lays the foundation for understanding how stars forge the elements we are made of and scatter them across the heavens when they explode.

  10. The Big Splat, or How Our Moon Came to Be

    Dana Mackenzie

    Why is our Moon so special? Where did it come from, and why is it so large compared with Earth, unlike the moons of the other planets? The Big Splat answers these questions and more.

  11. The History and Practice of Ancient Astronomy

    James Evans

    How did we get the first calendars? How did watching the Moon, planets and stars shape our ancient ancestors’ sense of their place in the Universe? Here are the stories of the instruments they built and the observations they made, many still with us today.

  12. The Observing Guide to the Messier Marathon

    Don Machholz

    The granddaddy of observing challenges for the citizen scientist-astronomer: catching every one of the 110 objects in Messier’s catalogue in a single night. This is how you pull it off.

  13. The Origin and Evolution of Planetary Nebulae

    Sun Kwok

    When Sun-like stars “die,” they don’t explode. They shed their outer layers into a glowing cloud of gas and dust that reminded early astronomers of a planet’s disk, each one beautifully unique, from the Ring Nebula to the Helix and the Eskimo Nebula. This book explains what happens.

  14. The Transits of Venus

    William Sheehan and John Westfall

    How do you measure distances in the solar system with any precision? It turns out that when Venus crosses the face of the Sun, you can work it out. This book tells the story of how we did just that.

  15. Webb Society Deep Sky Observer’s Handbook

    Kenneth Glyn Jones

    If you ever get a smart telescope or another good-sized scope, here’s a guide to what to expect, written by amateurs for amateurs.
